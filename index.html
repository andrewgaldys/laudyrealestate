What sets value on Fort Lauderdale water

Two homes with the same square footage can trade far apart because of what happens between the dock and the Atlantic. These are the variables Nick checks first, in the order they tend to rule a property in or out.

Read the full guide to evaluating boating access

  1. Route to the ocean

    A fixed bridge sets a hard ceiling on air draft; a drawbridge trades height for schedule. The SE 17th Street bridge, the last Intracoastal crossing north of Port Everglades, clears 55 feet at mean high water when closed and otherwise opens on a Coast Guard schedule.

    Source: U.S. Coast Guard, Federal Register (2025)

  2. Controlling depth

    The shallowest point between the dock and the inlet at low water decides which hulls can use a home. Deep water at the seawall means little if the canal mouth is shallow.

  3. Usable frontage

    Linear feet of seawall matter less than what the city lets you berth. Fort Lauderdale generally limits mooring structures to 25 percent of the waterway's width or 25 feet, whichever is less, and vessels may not extend past the side setback lines.

    Source: Fort Lauderdale ULDR Sec. 47-19.3

  4. Seawall elevation and condition

    In 2023 the city raised its required seawall height to 5.0 feet NAVD88 for new walls and substantial repairs. A wall that must be raised or rebuilt is a capital cost a buyer will price in.

    Source: City of Fort Lauderdale seawall ordinance (2023)

  5. Price, adjusted for the water

    Comparable sales only compare when they share a bridge constraint, depth, frontage, and wall condition. Price per square foot hides most of what a waterfront buyer is paying for.

Five neighborhoods, five different trade-offs

Each of these east Fort Lauderdale neighborhoods is strongest at something different. The comparison guide sets them side by side on walkability, dockage, and lot size.

  • Las Olas Isles

    Finger isles off East Las Olas Boulevard: walkable to dining, nearly every home on the water, and no fixed bridges between the isles and the ocean.

  • Harbor Beach

    Deep-water estates beside Port Everglades, with an association-funded security patrol and a private beach club. The most private of the five, and the least walkable.

  • Coral Ridge

    A broad mix of waterfront and dry lots between Sunrise and Oakland Park Boulevards. Water access depends heavily on which side of Bayview Drive a home sits.

  • Rio Vista

    One of the city's oldest neighborhoods, on the south bank of the New River: tree canopy, varied lots, and river, canal, and Intracoastal frontage.

  • Victoria Park

    Walkable and close to Las Olas and downtown, with compact lots and limited waterfront along its eastern canals.

Who Nick works with

The questions change with the client. The discipline does not: measure what decides value before debating what does not.

Boat-first buyers

Buyers who start with a vessel, not a floor plan. The search filters on air draft against every fixed bridge on the route, draft against controlling depth, and length and beam against what the city allows at the dock.

Luxury buyers and sellers

For estate homes where comparable sales are few and the differences sit in the water, the lot, and the permits, pricing starts from matched comparables rather than price per square foot.

Relocating from the Northeast

Florida has no state personal income tax, and a homestead exemption with the Save Our Homes cap can change the long-run cost of a primary residence. Two things surprise buyers: property taxes are reassessed to market value after a sale, so the seller's bill is not your bill, and insurance and flood coverage should be quoted before an offer, not after.

Oceanfront condo buyers

Florida requires condominium buildings three or more habitable stories tall to complete milestone structural inspections and structural integrity reserve studies. Nick reads those reports, the reserve funding plan, and any special-assessment history alongside the view.

Investors and developers

Buildable value on the water depends on flood elevation requirements, seawall obligations, dock limits, and zoning. Nick frames those constraints early, so an offer reflects the project that can be permitted.
