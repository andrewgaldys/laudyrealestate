How to evaluate boating access on a Fort Lauderdale waterfront home

A home on the water is only as useful as the route from its dock to the ocean. This guide works through the variables in the order they usually decide a property: bridges, depth, frontage, and the seawall, then how each one shows up in price.

Written by Nick Napoli. Last reviewed September 30, 2026.

The short version

  • A fixed bridge is a hard ceiling. Compare your air draft with the closed clearance of every fixed bridge between the dock and the inlet, and leave margin for high tides.
  • Drawbridges trade height for time. The SE 17th Street bridge, the last Intracoastal crossing north of Port Everglades, clears 55 feet at mean high water when closed.
  • Controlling depth, the shallowest point on the route at low water, matters more than depth at the dock.
  • Frontage is limited by the canal. Fort Lauderdale generally caps mooring structures at 25 percent of a waterway's width or 25 feet, whichever is less.
  • Seawalls carry cost. New and substantially repaired seawalls in Fort Lauderdale must reach 5.0 feet NAVD88.

Start with the route to the ocean

Fort Lauderdale has about 165 miles of navigable waterways within the city, and they are not interchangeable. The first question for any waterfront address is what stands between the dock and open water, and whether your boat fits under, through, or over it.

Fixed bridges: the hard ceiling

A fixed bridge does not open. If your air draft plus a sensible margin is greater than its clearance at the tide you will be running, the route does not work for that boat. Listings often say "no fixed bridges," which usually means no fixed bridge between the property and the ocean. Confirm it for the specific canal, because conditions can change from one street to the next.

Coral Ridge shows why. Many homes east of Bayview Drive reach the Intracoastal directly, while homes on the Middle River side can sit behind a fixed bridge. Same neighborhood, different boat.

Drawbridges: clearance on a schedule

Most Intracoastal crossings in Fort Lauderdale are bascule bridges, or drawbridges. They open, but on schedules the U.S. Coast Guard sets in its drawbridge operation regulations (33 CFR Part 117), and those schedules can include rush-hour restrictions that change over time. The SE 17th Street (Brooks Memorial) bridge, the last Intracoastal crossing north of Port Everglades, has a 55-foot vertical clearance at mean high water in the closed position. A boat that clears it closed never waits; a taller one plans its day around openings.

Rail bridges on the New River

Properties upriver on the New River also have to account for the Florida East Coast Railway bridge downtown, a single-leaf bascule with about four feet of clearance when closed. It stays open except when lowered for rail traffic, inspections, or maintenance, and federal rules cap each closure at 60 consecutive minutes. For an upriver address, that bridge belongs in the time-to-ocean estimate.

Time to the inlet

Distance is only part of it. Idle and slow-speed zones, some of them seasonal manatee protection zones, combine with bridge schedules and weekend traffic to set the real run time. Most central Fort Lauderdale addresses run to Port Everglades; homes farther north may weigh other inlets depending on conditions and the route.

Route checklist for a specific address

  • List every bridge between the dock and the inlet, fixed or bascule, with its closed clearance.
  • Measure air draft with antennas, outriggers, and hardtop in the position you will actually run.
  • Check the operating schedule and any current restrictions for each drawbridge.
  • Note idle and slow-speed zones on the route, including seasonal ones.
  • Estimate run time at the hour you would actually head out.

Depth: find the controlling number

Depth at the seawall is the number most buyers ask for and the least useful on its own. What matters is the controlling depth: the shallowest water between the dock and the inlet at low tide. A home with deep water at the wall and a shoal at the canal mouth has the depth of the shoal.

Draft plus margin

Compare your draft with the controlling depth at a low-water datum, then leave room for below-average tides, silt, and the way a hull settles lower underway. How much margin is enough depends on the boat and how you run it. The point is to decide it before an offer, not after the first low tide.

Where depth data comes from

  • Recent sounding or hydrographic surveys, referenced to a stated low-water datum.
  • Dredging history and permits for the canal, if any.
  • NOAA charts and tide predictions for the route, which show general depths but not every residential canal.
  • Local knowledge, confirmed by measurement.

Tides move both limits

The same high tide that lifts a hull off a shoal lowers the clearance under a bridge. Seasonal king tides, typically in the fall, push both limits at once, which is why margin matters in both directions.

Frontage: what you can actually berth

Linear feet of seawall is a starting point. Usable frontage is what remains after the city's rules and the canal's geometry have their say.

Canal width sets the limit

Under Fort Lauderdale's Unified Land Development Regulations (Sec. 47-19.3), docks and other mooring structures generally may not extend into a waterway more than 25 percent of its width or 25 feet, whichever is less. Mooring and dolphin piles are limited to 30 percent of the width or 25 feet. Extensions beyond those limits need city approval, and the code caps them at 30 percent of the waterway's width. On a narrow canal, that arithmetic decides how wide a beam you can berth.

Side setbacks shape vessel length

Vessels docked at a residential property generally may not extend beyond the side setback lines required for the house, projected into the water. On a lot with 100 feet of frontage, the longest boat you can berth along the seawall is shorter than 100 feet once those setbacks apply. There are local exceptions (the code carves out Isle of Venice and Hendricks Isle, for example), so the rule has to be read for the specific address.

Where the lot sits on the water

  • Point and wide-water lots: more frontage and longer views, often easier maneuvering, and usually priced accordingly.
  • Intracoastal frontage: direct access and big views, with more wake and traffic exposure.
  • Interior canal lots: calmer water; check width, turning room, and the route out.
  • End-of-canal lots: privacy, with turning room and water circulation worth checking.

Dock infrastructure

Record what exists and whether it was permitted: dock type, lift capacity, shore power service, water, and lighting. Unpermitted improvements become the buyer's problem at closing, and the buyer's leverage before it.

Seawalls: elevation, condition, and cost

A seawall holds the lot in place and keeps tidal water out. In Fort Lauderdale it is also a regulated elevation.

The elevation standard

In 2023 the City of Fort Lauderdale raised its required seawall height from 3.9 feet to 5.0 feet NAVD88. The standard applies to new seawalls and to substantial repairs, and the city can cite walls low enough to let tidal water flood neighboring property or streets; cited owners have a year to comply. Broward County's countywide tidal flood barrier standard also sets 5.0 feet NAVD88, with a 2050 deadline.

Reading condition

  • Cracks, spalling, or exposed reinforcing steel in the cap and panels.
  • Leaning, bowing, or separation at joints.
  • Soil loss behind the wall: depressions, sinkholes, or settling pavers.
  • Condition of tie-backs, weep holes, and the cap.
  • Surveyed cap elevation in NAVD88, compared with the current standard.

What drives cost

Seawall work is quoted by the linear foot, but the drivers are specific to the site: wall length, construction method (a new wall in front of the old one or a full replacement), access by land or barge, removing and reinstalling the dock and lift, and permitting at the city, county, and sometimes state or federal level. Get contractor estimates for the specific wall; averages from other markets do not transfer.

How the water shows up in price

Waterfront pricing in Fort Lauderdale is a set of adjustments layered on top of the house. Price per square foot hides most of them. Here is how each variable tends to move value, and how Nick accounts for it.

Water variables and their effect on value
Variable Why buyers pay for it How Nick accounts for it
Route to the ocean No fixed bridges opens a home to the widest pool of boat owners, including sailboats and tall sportfishing boats. Compares sales with the same bridge constraint; a fixed-bridge home is not a comparable for an open-route one.
Controlling depth Deep water admits larger, deeper-draft vessels without planning around the tide. Documents depth along the route, not only at the wall.
Usable frontage Frontage decides the size and number of boats; wide water adds views and room to maneuver. Measures berthing capacity under city rules rather than raw linear feet.
Seawall A wall at 5.0 feet NAVD88 in good condition removes a large near-term expense. Treats a wall that needs raising or replacement as a buyer's cost, supported by estimates.
Dock and permits Permitted docks, lifts, and power are usable on the first day. Confirms permits and records; unpermitted work is priced as risk.
Lot position Point and wide-water lots are scarce. Adjusts for position and exposure: wake, traffic, and views.

The practical consequence is that two houses with identical interiors can trade far apart because of a bridge, a shoal, or a seawall. For sellers, documenting these variables before listing (survey, wall inspection, permits, and route data) answers buyer questions before they turn into price negotiations.

Common questions

What does "no fixed bridges" mean on a Fort Lauderdale listing?

Usually that no fixed, non-opening bridge stands between the property and the ocean, so vessel height is limited only by drawbridge schedules and the inlet. It does not mean there are no bridges at all, and it should be confirmed for the specific canal.

Is Intracoastal frontage better than a canal lot?

It depends on the boat and the owner. Intracoastal lots usually have direct access and wide views, along with more wake and traffic. Canal lots are calmer; their value turns on width, depth, and the route out.

How deep should the water be for my boat?

Deeper than your draft at the shallowest point of the route at low water, with a margin you have decided on for your hull and how you run it. Ask for depth along the route, not only at the dock.

Do I have to raise an existing seawall?

Not automatically. In Fort Lauderdale the 5.0-foot NAVD88 standard applies to new walls and substantial repairs, and a wall that lets tidal water flow onto neighboring property can be cited. Whether a given wall triggers work is a question for a survey and the city.

Can I dock a boat longer than my frontage?

Generally not beyond the side setback lines extended into the water, though specific locations have exceptions. The canal's width also limits how far a vessel may extend from the seawall.
